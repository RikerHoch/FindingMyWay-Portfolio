Description
Original Description - Discussion Board:

In our week 13 activity focused on using information ethically, specifically practicing on how to paraphrase and cite sources correctly when conducting research. the topic chosen by our group was open-source software, covering areas such as what open source software is, how it compares to closed source software, the risks associated with it and why someone might choose it. We utilized Frosty Mod Manager & Frosty Editor as a real world example of open source software to ground our research and compiled a list in the APA format to backup our findings.

Updated Description - Portfolio:

In week 13 our group conducted a research activity into open-source software as part of the activity primarily focused on the ethical use of information. The task required us to research, paraphrase and cite sources correctly in the APA format. While covering key areas of the topic including: "what open source software is, how it differs from its closed source software relatives, its associated risks and the reasons a user or developer may choose to use it". Frosty Mod manager and Frosty Editor were both prime examples to anchor the research in something concrete and applicable.

Reflection
Original Reflection - Discussion Board:

Analysis:

The most challenging aspect of this activity was paraphrasing without falling into patchwriting, which is where you swap out a few words from the original source without truly rewriting the idea in your own words. When researching open source software I found myself initially restructuring sentences too closely to the original material than processing that same information and expressing it through my own opinion. This required me to slow down and genuinely understand the content before writing, rather than treating the source as a template.

Insight:

My understanding of citation also shifted thr4ough the session I had preciously treated references as a formality rather than an acknowledgement of where the ideas originated. seeing how sources directly shaped the structure of our research mase it clearer that citation is about intellectual honesty as much as it is avoiding plagiarism.

Future Action:

As an IT professional, theses skills will be directly relevant when writing technical documentation, reports or drawing on existing software and research. Moving forward I will make a conscious effort to completely rewrite the material through my own personal analysis of the material.

Original Reflection - Peer Feedback:

Aliaa Farag:

Your reflection shows a clear understanding of ethical use of information, especially the difference between paraphrasing and patchwriting. I also agree with your point that paraphrasing requires real understanding rather than just changing words, because it is easy to stay too close to the original source without realizing it.

I like how you connected citation to intellectual honesty, not just an academic requirement. This is important because it changes how we see research work, especially when using multiple sources in a group project. Your example of open-source software also made the discussion more practical and easier to understand.

One thing I would add is that patchwriting is often a step in learning, especially when dealing with new technical topics. Over time, with more practice, it becomes easier to break away from the structure of the original text and form your own academic voice.

Do you think using tools like note summaries before writing could help reduce patchwriting in future research tasks, especially in technical subjects like IT?

Luka Kelly:

You reflection for this week is very good, you have shown where you have learnt and where you had challenges. I understand how falling into patchwork is very easy to do on accident. One area to improve could be to explain why paraphrasing without falling into patchwork was difficult. A strength you have in this reflection is your insight, you clearly show where your thinking has changed and what you have learned.

Updated Reflection - Portfolio:

Analysis - Updated:

Working through this activity exposed a gap in my research process that I had not previously identified. My initial instinct when paraphrasing technical content was to stay close to the initial source material, adjusting wording without restructuring the idea independently. This activity assisted me in realizing that what I was doing was patchwriting not paraphrasing, it revealed that my reading and writhing had become disconnected I was processing text visually rather than intellectually. The shift required was to move away from reading and writing to reading processing and writing it in my own expression rather than just writing down what is seen. For a technical subject like open source software where precise terminology matters, this distinction becomes especially important because misrepresenting a concept through poor paraphrasing carries real consequences in a professional context.

  • Analysis - [Group Element - Feedback Based: Aliaa Farag]

Aliaa affirmed that the connection I drew between citation and intellectual honesty, noting that this perspective is particularly relevant when multiple contributors are working from different sources withing a group project. She also offered a reframe that i found genuinely useful, suggesting that patchwriting is not necessarily a failure but a rather natural stage in in developing my own academic voice, particularly when engaging with unfamiliar technical material. this shifted my thinking away from treating it as an error to be corrected and toward treating it as diagnostic information about where my understanding the material was still incomplete.

  • Analysis - [Group Element - Feedback Based: Luka Kelly]

Luka defined a key element missing reflection the could hold a critical point between a bad reflection and a good one where I should mention "why paraphrasing without falling into patchwork was difficult?". this can often be seen as difficult because while writing its easy to follow directly from a source while adding my own influence however it is more challenging to read a source and then just rewrite it how I would imagine it to be in my head this is what makes it s difficult to paraphrase for me.

Insight - Updated:

This activity reframed citation for me in a way that will carry over into my professional practice. Referencing had previously felt like a compliance step, something done at the end to satisfy the requirement. What became clear throughout this session is that citation is a form of transparency, it documents intellectual pathway behind a piece of work and allows others evaluate the works viability and reliability of what would be produced. In an IT professional context this maps directly onto practices like version control, audit trails and documented sources in technical reports however the underlying principle remains unaltered: Showing your work and attributing influence is what makes any output trustworthy and verifiable.

Future Action - Updated:

Going forward I will restructure how I engage with sources before writing rather than trying to paraphrase better in the moment. Specifically I will read a source, set it aside and rebuild the core idea from memory before writing a single sentence. This forces a genuine comprehension and naturally prevents patchwriting from occurring in the first place. Beyond the academic context I see this habit directly applicable to how I will handle technical research in an IT role, whether that is reviewing documentation, drawing on existing solutions or producing reports that require independent thinking about a particular topic.

In week 1 we explored the subject of climate change as a wicked problem and as far as common knowledge goes a wicked problem is complicated, ongoing and contains no single solution proven to be effective in dealing with these kinds of problems.

Climate change as a whole is often influenced by many different factors such as industrial production, greenhouse gas emissions, fossil fuel consumption, deforestation  and global economic systems. Because of these overlapping causes it cannot be solved directly through a simple action or policy change.

Updated Description - Portfolio:

For the first week of finding my way our group was tasked with discussing climate changed as a wicked problem which is a kind of problem defined by its complexity and its resistance to a straight forward solution and its tendency to evolve rather than be solved. What distinguishes a wicked problem from a complicated one is that there is no definitive answer, only a series that carry their own trade offs and consequences. Climate change fits this particular definition because its root cause is not directly isolated. Industrial pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, fossil fuel, deforestation are all factoring contributors to climate change simultaneously, meaning the problem cannot be address by an isolated factor  "if one factor exists then the problem exists"

Please submit a complete project proposal document as a single PDF file via the Canvas system on the due date at the latest.

Your submission should be professionally formatted, with clear section headings, appropriate use of diagrams and tables, and proper citation of any referenced materials. The body of your proposal should be between 1200 and 1500 ± 10% words, excluding the cover page, table of contents, diagrams, and references, and team collaboration statement.

Your work should demonstrate academic integrity. Use APA 7th edition referencing to acknowledge all external sources in your report, in line with the guidelines below.  

Submission instructions

  1. Save your document as: StudentID1_StudentID2_ProjectProposal.pdf (e.g., for a paired work, the filename could be 12345678_23456789_ProjectProposal.pdf).
  2. Upload your file via the "Assessment 1 Submission" portal in the Canvas system.
  3. Ensure the file is under 10MB.
  4. Only PDF format is accepted; Word documents, Google Docs links, or other formats will not be marked.

Submission checklist

Before submitting, ensure your proposal includes all required components:

Cover Page:

  • Project title (clear and descriptive)
  • Student name(s) and ID(s)
  • Supervisor name (course lecturer)
  • Course code and name
  • Submission date
  • Declaration of AI tool use (if applicable): "We used [tool name] to [what you used it for]" OR "We did not use AI tools for this assessment"

Table of Contents:

  • List all sections with page numbers

Part 1: Abstract

  • Overview of the problem
  • Brief mention of existing solutions
  • Summary of your proposed solution
  • Expected outcomes/benefits

Part 2: Introduction

  • Detailed explanation of the problem: What is the problem? Why does it matter? Who experiences this problem?
  • Analysis of 1-2 existing solutions or similar products: What do they do? What features do they have? What do you like about them? What could be better?
  • SMART aims and objectives for your project

Part 3: Proposed Solution

  • Clear description of your solution: What will your program/app do? Who will use it? How will people use it (describe the main actions users will take)? Main features you plan to include.
  • What you will NOT include (set boundaries)
  • Visualisations showing the main parts of your solution: Example: flow chart or block diagram showing how the program works, or sketch of what screens/menus users will see
  • Technology choices with explanation: What will you use?
  • Why these choices?

Part 4: Stakeholder Analysis

  • Identification of relevant stakeholders
  • Roles and interests
  • Power/Interest grid (or similar analysis tool)
  • How stakeholder needs will be addressed 

Part 5: Project Plan

  • Project timeline with milestones (using Gantt chart or tables)
  • Resource requirements (software, tools, hardware)
  • Risk assessment (at least three risks and mitigation strategies)
  • Team roles and responsibilities: Member A responsibilities. Member B responsibilities. What you'll do together.

Part 6: References

  • Minimum of five academic or professional sources
  • Correct APA 7th edition formatting

Part 7: Team Collaboration Statement

  • How did you divide the work for this proposal? Who wrote which sections? What did you do together?
  • How did you communicate and meet?
  • How many times did you meet/video call?
  • Did you use shared documents?
  • Is the work split fairly?
  • Both team members must sign: "We confirm this statement is accurate and we both contributed to this proposal."

Appendices (if applicable)

  • Further diagrams, charts, or supporting materials
  • Evidence of initial research (optional)

Total main content: 1200-1500 ± 10% words.

  Referencing

​​​Referencing is used to give credit to ideas presented in your work that are not your own. This means that you can quote or paraphrase from your research as long as you acknowledge where those words and ideas came from. If you do not correctly reference your work, you might have it rejected for plagiarism.

Depending on your school, you might be asked to reference in APA or Chicago style.
